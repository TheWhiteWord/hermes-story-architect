## What a scene is

**A scene is a story in miniature** — an action through conflict in a
continuity of time and space that turns the value-charged condition of a
character's life. Ideally every scene is an event.

- **A scene turns or it is nothing.** Compare the charge on the value at
  scene opening with the charge at scene closing. If they are the same, the
  scene is a nonevent, and the question is why it is necessary at all.
- **The turn is the scene.** Not the largest event, not the most dramatic
  one — the point at which the value is different on the way out than on the
  way in.
- **No scene that doesn't turn** is the ideal, not an absolute. A flat scene
  can be a deliberate rest, but then it is a decision and not an oversight.

## The dynamics of a scene

1. The character acts on the scene's objective — the immediate desire, in this
   time and this place — under pressure.
2. The conflict, from one of the concentric levels, answers unexpectedly.
3. **The gap** opens between what was expected and what came.
4. The value-charged condition turns, positive to negative or the reverse.

The scene's objective is an aspect of the super-objective — the spine, the
story-long quest from the inciting incident to the climax.

## Beats

**A beat is an exchange of behavior in action and reaction**, and beats are
the building blocks a scene's turn is made of.

- **A beat continues until behavior changes.** One action, one reaction; a new
  beat starts when the character acts differently.
- **Action and reaction are both described as active gerund phrases** — on the
  surface and on the underlying level at once — so that the emotional
  connotation is carried rather than a literal description.
- **Surveying the beats reveals the shape of the scene.** Even behavior that
  looks random has a design. The moment the largest gap opens between
  expectation and result is the turning point.

## Text and subtext

**Text is the sensory surface** — what is explicitly shown or said. **Subtext
is the life under that surface** — thoughts and feelings, known and unknown,
hidden by behavior.

- Nothing is what it seems; a character veils the truth with a living mask.
- Writing holds both layers at once: the sensory surface, and the inner world
  of desires, actions, reactions, impulses and instincts behind it.
- **A scene on the surface alone is exposition.** Dramatized exposition
  presents facts through scene, dialogue and behavior rather than explaining
  them.

## What the audience knows

Three positions, each a choice about what the audience watches a character
find out:

- **Mystery** — the audience knows less than the characters.
- **Suspense** — the audience and the characters know the same.
- **Dramatic irony** — the audience knows more than the characters.

- **Exposition earns the audience's attention before it spends it.** Backstory
  enters as a live scene at a critical moment, not as explanation — a
  flashback is not brought in until the audience has a need and desire to
  know. Bringing one earlier is withholding, not mystery.
