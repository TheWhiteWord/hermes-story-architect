## The unit underneath a scene

**A story event creates meaningful change in a character's life situation,
expressed and experienced in terms of a value.** That is the basic unit of a
story, and a scene is an attempt to be one.

- **A scene turns the value-charged condition of a character's life**, on at
  least one value, with perceptible significance. Ideally every scene is an
  event.
- **The test is a comparison.** What value is at stake, what is its charge at
  the opening, what is its charge at the closing, and has it shifted?
- **If it has not shifted, the scene needs a reason to exist** — and the
  honest answer is that it does not. A scene without a turn is a nonevent, a
  flat scene, and exposition only.
- "No scene that doesn't turn" is the rule, not a preference. A scene that
  does not turn has not failed to be dramatic; it has failed to be a scene.

## What a scene is

**A scene is a story in miniature** — an action driven by conflict, occurring
in continuous time and space, that turns the value.

- **It is bounded.** Continuous time and space: not a summary of a week, and
  not two places stitched together. Everything in it happens in one stretch of
  one place.
- **The turn is the scene.** Not the loudest event, not the largest one — the
  point at which the value is different on the way out than on the way in.
- **Its objective is an aspect of the spine.** The objective is the immediate
  desire, in this time and this place; the spine is the story-long quest from
  the inciting incident to the climax, and every scene's objective is a piece
  of it.

## How a scene runs

1. Someone drives the scene and motivates the actions in it.
2. They act on the objective, under pressure.
3. The conflict answers — from one of the concentric levels: inner, personal,
   or extra-personal.
4. **The gap** opens between what was expected and what came.
5. The value-charged condition turns, positive to negative or the reverse.

And the same walk is how a written scene is read back: name who drives it,
note the value and its charge at the opening, break the scene into beats on
both the surface and the underlying level, note the charge at the closing and
compare, then survey the beats and locate the turning point — the moment the
largest gap opened.

**This is where scenes go wrong.** A scene may lack conflict, be repetitive,
have its turning point in the wrong place, or lack credibility. Credibility is
the one worth naming: *on the nose* dialogue — a character stating the thing
that is true of them, instead of behaving it. Analysis exists to find these
and guide the rewrite.

## Beats

**A beat is an exchange of behavior in action and reaction**, and beats are the
building blocks that shape the turning of a scene.

- **A beat continues until behavior changes.** One action, one reaction; the
  next beat starts when the character acts differently.
- **Action and reaction are named as active gerund phrases** — on the surface
  and on the underlying level at once — so the emotional connotation is
  carried instead of a literal description.
- **Surveying the beats reveals the shape of the scene.** Even behavior that
  looks random has a design, and the moment the largest gap opened between
  expectation and result is the turning point.

## Text and subtext

**Text is the sensory surface** — what is explicitly shown or said. **Subtext
is the life under that surface** — thoughts and feelings, known and unknown,
hidden by behavior.

- Nothing is what it seems; a character veils the truth with a living mask.
- Writing holds both layers at once: the sensory surface, and the inner world
  of desires, actions, reactions, impulses and instincts behind it.
- **Exposition enters as scene, not as explanation.** Necessary facts are
  presented through scene, dialogue and behavior.

## What the audience knows

Three positions, each a deliberate choice about what the audience watches a
character find out:

- **Mystery** — the audience knows less than the characters.
- **Suspense** — the audience and the characters know the same.
- **Dramatic irony** — the audience knows more than the characters.

- **Exposition earns the audience's attention before it spends it.** Backstory
  arrives as a live scene at a critical moment, not as summary — a flashback
  is not brought in until the audience has a need and desire to know.
  Bringing one earlier is withholding, not mystery.
