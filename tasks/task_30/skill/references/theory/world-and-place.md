## Setting is four-dimensional

1. **Period** — the story's place in history.
2. **Duration** — the length of the story's timeframe.
3. **Location** — the story's place in space.
4. **Level of conflict** — the story's position on the hierarchy of human
   struggles: inner, personal, or extra-personal.

## The world constrains what can happen

**All fine stories take place within a limited, knowable world** — small
enough for the writer to command it completely, not in triviality but in
knowability: knowledge of everything germane to the story.

- **A story must adhere to its own internal logic and probability.** Once the
  audience understands the rules of the reality, breaking them reads as
  illogical and unconvincing. That is the audience contract.
- **The writer's event choices are constrained** by the possibilities and
  probabilities of the world as established.
- **The smaller and better known the world, the greater the choice and the
  more original the result.** A larger world dilutes the writer's knowledge,
  reduces the choices available, and produces cliché.

## Cliché

**Cliché is rooted in the audience's dissatisfaction and in not knowing the
world of the story.** It comes from reaching for what is already familiar
because the real material has not been dug out.

- **The war on cliché is research**, of three kinds: **memory** (personal
  experience relevant to the characters' lives), **imagination** (empathetic
  immersion in a character's daily life), and **fact** (external research into
  the world and the genre).
- **Research feeds invention; it is not the point.** A story is a design of
  events, not accumulated information.

## What a world holds

Before the story can be designed, the world it happens in is defined:

- **Livelihood** — how the characters earn a living.
- **Power** — who holds power and how, in society, in relationships, even in a
  household.
- **Rituals** — the customs and practices of the world.
- **Values** — concepts of good and evil, right and wrong, and the laws; the
  moral, the ethical and the legal are distinct from each other.
- **Genre** — the genre or combination of genres, and the conventions that
  come with it: specific settings, roles, events and values.
- **Biographies and backstory** — the life histories of the characters, and
  the significant past events among them.

## The image system

**An image system is a strategy of motifs** — a category of imagery embedded
in the story that repeats in sight and sound, as subliminal communication, to
increase the depth of the feeling rather than stating it.

- It works by **subtlety, variety and persistence**. A motif repeated
  obviously is a symbol announced; a motif that returns in different forms is
  the argument being made underneath the scenes.
- **Imagery ascends.** A story's images start literal and particular, and
  progress toward the universal and the archetypal — a thing that is only
  itself at the beginning and comes to stand for the human condition by the
  end. The place is where that ascent happens, because a place is what can be
  seen.
- So a place is not a backdrop. It is the most durable carrier of the story's
  subtext, because a motif can be planted in a room and cashed in a different
  one, months apart, and the audience will feel the connection without being
  told.

## Showing a world, not stating it

**The world is the thing most often explained directly, and explaining it is
the failure.** History, laws, customs and the shape of a society are the
easiest material in a story to put in a paragraph, and the paragraph is what
kills it.

- **Necessary facts are presented indirectly**, through natural scene,
  dialogue and behavior. A world is shown by what people do under its rules,
  not by a description of the rules.
- **Exposition is a placement problem, not a writing problem.** The fact
  enters when the audience has a need for it, and in a form that cannot be
  ignored — otherwise it is the writer talking, and the story stops while
  they do.

## The world as antagonist

- **The world of the story is a cosmology**: structure, stripped of surface
  detail, reveals a writer's understanding of the world's order, motivations
  and patterns. It is a map of life's hidden order.
- **The environment is an antagonist in its own right** — time, space and the
  objects in them, man-made and natural, are the outermost circle of
  conflict.
- **The world's belief and the world's reality are separate things**, and the
  break between them is where the story happens. The characters act on what
  they believe is true; the world answers as it is.
- **Know the world in order to write against it.** A cliché is what happens
  when the writer reaches for the audience's prior knowledge instead of the
  world's own.
