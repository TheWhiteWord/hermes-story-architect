## What a value is

**A value is a universal quality of human experience that can shift between
positive and negative states from moment to moment.** Alive/dead, love/hate,
freedom/slavery, truth/lie, courage/cowardice, loyalty/betrayal,
wisdom/stupidity, hope/despair — the last being value-charged rather than
moral.

- **A value is what a story is written around.** What is worth living for,
  what is worth dying for, what pursuits are foolish, the meaning of justice
  and truth. A writer's task is to uncover insight into these and communicate
  it.
- **Change in a value is the only proof.** An event creates meaningful change
  in terms of a value; a change that is not in terms of a value is activity.

## The opposition between values

**A protagonist and the story are only as intellectually fascinating and
emotionally compelling as the forces of antagonism make them.** Values do not
merely sit opposite each other; they stand in a ladder of opposition.

- **Positive** — the ideal state.
- **Contrary** — the opposite, but not its absolute negation.
- **Contradictory** — the direct negation of the positive.
- **Negation of negation** — the negation of the contradictory, appearing as a
  perversion of, or false version of, the positive.

**The relationships run both ways, and which one you use is the whole
difference in depth:**

- positive ↔ contrary — opposition, tension
- positive → negation of negation — the positive leads to its distorted form
- contrary → contradictory — the contrary leads to its direct opposite
- negation of negation → positive — the distorted form leads back

- **Depth requires the movement through them.** An opposition that stays at
  the contrary level is tension. Reaching the contradictory level, or arriving
  at the negation of negation and returning, is what a story is about.
- **The schema is per value.** Each value has its own worked schema — the
  instances where a particular positive becomes a particular distortion of
  itself. It is not a generic pattern to fill in.

## The controlling idea

**A story argues.** It builds a bridge from a premise to a controlling idea —
expressed in a single sentence describing how and why life changes from
beginning to end — through a dialectical debate between the positive idea and
its negative counter-idea. The climax resolves that debate, and the resolution
is the controlling idea.

- **Both sides get equal power.** The sequences and scenes arguing against the
  final statement are as truthful and energetic as those supporting it.
  A one-sided story is didactic, not dialectical.
- **The idea is proved, not explained.** Structure demonstrates the idea
  through events, without explanation. A writer with an idea they cannot
  convert into action has had a creative defeat.
- **A controlling idea has one of three shapes**, and the story's arc
  matches it: an *idealistic* controlling idea is an up-ending story, a
  *pessimistic* one a down-ending, and an *ironic* one holds both at once —
  a complex, dual view of existence, life at its most complete and realistic.

## Where a value is measured

- **A story** compares the charge at open and close; the arc is the sweep, and
  the final change must be absolute and irreversible.
- **A scene** records what is at stake, the charge entering, the charge
  leaving, and the turn between them. No scene that doesn't turn — a scene
  with no change is either a nonevent or an error, and the difference has to
  be a decision.
- **A character** carries a value that may differ from the story's, and the
  arc is the sweep of that value's charge for them alone.
