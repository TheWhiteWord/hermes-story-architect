## Change comes in degrees

Each level of structure exists to carry a size of value change, and the sizes
are not the same size.

- **A scene** turns the value-charged condition of a character's life.
- **A sequence** is a series of scenes culminating in one with greater impact
  than any preceding scene.
- **An act** is a series of sequences peaking in a climactic scene, causing a
  major reversal of values, more powerful than any previous sequence or scene.
- **A story** is a series of acts culminating in absolute and irreversible
  change. The story's arc is the sweep from the initial to the final
  condition, and that final change cannot be undone.
- **A beat** is the smallest: an exchange of behavior in action and reaction,
  the building block a scene's turn is made of. A beat continues until
  behavior changes.

## Why there is a climax at every level

**A climax is the culmination of a level, and it is defined by a comparison
with everything inside that level.** A sequence's climax is the scene with
greater impact than any preceding scene in it; an act's is the climax of that
act, more powerful than any previous sequence or scene; the story's delivers
the absolute and irreversible change.

- **The measure is relative, so it has to be taken at each level — and each
  level yields a different scene.** That is why climaxes repeat rather than
  there being one. It is a consequence of the comparison, not a convention
  layered on top.
- **The repetition mirrors the magnitude.** Scenes carry minor but significant
  change, sequences moderate and more impactful change, acts major reversals.
  A story runs three sizes of change, and a single climax could only mark the
  largest — leaving two thirds of the progression unmeasured.
- **Each is irreversible at its own scale.** The story climax is irreversible
  absolutely; an act climax is irreversible within its act; a sequence climax
  within its sequence. That is also why they are usually different scenes, and
  why a climax of one level is not thereby the climax of another.
- So the hierarchy of climaxes and the hierarchy of magnitudes are the same
  shape. Asked "where does the story turn hardest", the answer is the story's;
  asked "where does this act turn hardest", it is the act's, and both are
  correct at the scale they were asked.
- **They nest, so some coincide and most do not.** One climax per container at
  every level means the story has one climax, each act has one, and each
  sequence has one. Within an act, one of that act's sequence climaxes is the
  act's climax; the rest are only sequence climaxes. And of the act climaxes
  across the story, exactly one is also the story's. A scene can therefore
  carry several climaxes at once, or one, or — usually — none.

## Crisis, climax, resolution

- **A crisis is the true dilemma** at maximum pressure: the confrontation
  with the strongest antagonism, the last effort toward the desire. It is the
  obligatory scene — the audience wants to experience the struggle, so it is
  not avoided — and it is paused, a deliberate moment of choice, not a
  sequence of reactions.
- **A climax is a revolution in values**: a swing positive to negative or the
  reverse, with or without irony, at maximum charge, absolute and
  irreversible. It is where the change means something to the audience, and
  it is not the largest event — it is the one the value turns in hardest.
- **A resolution is the aftermath**: the consequences and fallout of the
  climax.

## Progression has modes

A story's movement can widen into the society, deepen into the intimate
relationships and the inner lives of its characters, rise from the particular
image to the universal one, or turn ironic.

- **Ironic progression** turns on the action producing the opposite of its
  intent: getting the desired thing, but too late; being pushed from the goal
  and led to it; throwing away the indispensable thing; steps toward the goal
  leading away from it; avoiding something miserable and finding happiness.
- **A story must not retreat.** It moves progressively forward, never
  returning to actions of lesser quality or magnitude.

## The spine

**The energy of the protagonist's desire is the spine of the story.** Every
scene, image and word relates to it, causally or thematically. If the
protagonist has no unconscious desire, the conscious objective is the spine.

## The inciting incident

**An event unbalances a character's life**, arousing conscious and/or
unconscious desire for restoration, and launching a quest for the object of
desire against antagonism. Success is not guaranteed.

- It **radically upsets the balance** of forces in the protagonist's life, and
  the protagonist must react to it.
- It **arouses the desire to restore balance**, and from that an object of
  desire is conceived — physical, situational, or attitudinal.
- It **raises the major dramatic question** and **projects the obligatory
  scene**.
- It comes early, but when the moment is ripe; it can be as small as a look
  across a table.
- It is **germane to the world, the characters and the genre**, and it arrives
  when the moment is ripe rather than arbitrarily early.

## The kind of story

Three designs, differing by what they reverse.

- **Classical**: cause and effect, an active goal-driven protagonist, a
  consistent reality, a single central character, external conflict, linear
  time, a closed ending where all questions are answered.
- **Minimalist**: a reduction of the classical. An open ending with questions
  left and emotions lingering, internal conflict emphasized, multiple
  protagonists, a passive protagonist who is reactive rather than proactive.
- **Antiplot**: a reversal of it. Coincidence instead of cause and effect,
  fragmented non-linear time, inconsistent realities, an open ending
  reflecting the disconnectedness of existence.

- **The structure and the characters are one decision.** A passive design in
  a classical structure is a contradiction, and a structure whose pressures
  do not fit its protagonist cannot be enacted. `character-and-arc.md` holds
  the character side: what the pressures are for.

## Structure and character are interlocked

The function of structure is to create progressively increasing pressures that
force characters into dilemmas and risk-taking choices, revealing their true
nature including its unconscious aspects. The function of character is to
embody the qualities needed to convincingly enact those choices. Changes in
one affect the other, and a design of one that ignores the other does not
work.
