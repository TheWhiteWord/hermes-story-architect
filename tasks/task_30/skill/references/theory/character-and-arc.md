## What a character is

**A character is a work of art — a metaphor for human nature.** Not a person
described, a shape of being expressed.

- **Archetype, not stereotype.** A universal human experience, expressed
  through specific, culturally particular detail. Originality comes from the
  confluence of content (who, what they want) and form (how it is told) — a
  change in one changes the other.
- **A vehicle for values.** Writers shape stories around what is worth living
  for, dying for, what is foolish, the meaning of justice and truth. A
  character is a means of investigating those, not a decoration on them.

## True character

**True character can only be expressed through choice in dilemma.** How a
person acts under pressure is who they are. Greater pressure, truer and deeper
choice. Character is revealed by what a person does when acting costs them
something.

- **Not by explanation.** Character is proven by event dynamics, not stated.
  Structure exists to force the revealing choice: progressively increasing
  pressure, forcing a character into dilemma and risk.
- **A choice between good and evil is no choice at all** — humans act
  according to their own perception of the right. A true choice is a dilemma:
  between irreconcilable goods, or between the lesser of two evils. It needs
  a three-sided situation, triangular the way life is, or there is no real
  decision to make.
- **A choice without risk is meaningless.** The pressure has to cost.
- **Credibility.** A character embodies the qualities needed to enact the
  choice convincingly — age, strength, worldview. Believability is a
  precondition, not a detail.
- **Change in inner nature, for better or worse.** A story that reveals
  character but never alters it has spent its central force on nothing.

## Contradiction

Character lives in contradiction. Flat traits are the failure, not the goal.

- **Internal contradiction** — guilt-ridden ambition.
- **Characterization vs deep character** — the charming thief. The surface
  and the core pull against each other.
- **Contradictions must be consistent** — a contradiction that resolves
  whenever convenient is a plot device, not a person.

## Desire

**The protagonist is a willful character**, defined by a clear conscious
desire, the capacities to pursue it, a plausible chance of attaining it, and
the will to pursue it to the human limit the setting and genre allow.

- **Unconscious desire is optional and may contradict the conscious one.**
  If there is none, the conscious objective is the spine.
- **Desire is measured by risk.** The value of what a character wants is in
  direct proportion to what they are willing to risk for it. A desire that
  costs nothing is not dramatically alive.
- **Empathy, not sympathy.** The audience must be able to understand a
  character — crawl inside their skin and see through their eyes. Liking them
  is not required.
- **Story lives at the extremes, not the middle.** The audience expects the
  limits to be reached.

## The gap

**Story is born where the subjective and objective realms touch.** A character
acts to get an expected positive result, and the world answers differently
than expected, more powerfully, or both. The break between expectation and
reality is where the human spirit meets the world.

- **The character is wrong about reality.** The gap is the discovery of that,
  in action.
- **The response is escalation.** The gap shows the minimal action was
  insufficient — struggle through, act more significantly, risk more. Then
  the pattern repeats at a higher risk, until a final action the audience
  cannot imagine following.
- **True action versus mere activity.** True action opens a gap and creates
  significant change. Activity produces the expected outcome and changes
  nothing. The two are not distinguished by effort but by consequence.
- **Writing from the inside out.** Get inside the character and inside
  yourself; the test at every moment is "if I were this character in these
  circumstances, what would I do?" Fine writing emphasizes reactions and the
  insight they produce, not events themselves.

## Levels of conflict

Conflict lives in concentric circles around the character's innermost self, and
the self is the center of experience. A character can be their own antagonist —
self-conflict is often the deepest source of drama.

1. **Inner** — mind, body, emotions. Conflict within the self.
2. **Personal** — intimate relationships: family, lovers, friends. Tension
   between social role and intimacy.
3. **Extra-personal** — the world beyond: institutions, roles, environment,
   time and space. The objective world, filtered through the subjective core.

- **Complexity is all three at once.** Complication is progressive
  accumulation; complexity is simultaneous presence. Complexity is the harder
  and richer of the two.
- **A character is defined by the levels they live in**, and by which one
  their struggle actually occupies.

## Change: the arc

**An arc is a change in inner nature over the course of the story.** Change is
always achieved through conflict; nothing changes without it.

- **The arc is a sweep of value charge.** A value is a universal quality of
  human experience that can shift positive or negative. The arc compares the
  charge at the beginning and at the end, and the final change must be
  absolute and irreversible.
- **The arc takes the shape of the story's controlling idea.** An up-ending
  story is idealistic, a down-ending pessimistic, and a story holding both at
  once ironic. A character whose arc cannot be stated in one of those terms is
  not yet shaped.
- **What forces the change is the structure** — the degrees of pressure, the
  size of each reversal, the crisis, the climax, the final action. Those are
  in `structure-and-plot.md`. The interlock is the point: a character
  designed apart from the structure that will pressure it is a design that
  cannot be enacted, and changing one requires changing the other.
- **The self can be the antagonist.** Inner conflict is conflict with oneself,
  and often the deepest there is.

## How the audience comes to know a character

**Emotion is made in an order, and a missing step kills it.** The audience
first empathizes with the character; then knows and supports what they want;
then understands the value at stake; only then does a change in that value
move them. Empathy without a known desire, or a change the audience can
locate, produces nothing.

- **Time with a character is what makes empathy** — every unit of it is an
  opportunity to witness a choice. A role the audience never sees decide
  anything is not a protagonist, whatever it is called.
- **You render the experience, not the emotion.** Give the audience what
  causes the feeling and it will feel it; naming the feeling achieves
  nothing. A scene's mood alone is not enough — the value has to move.

## The opposition a character meets

**A protagonist and the story are only as compelling as the forces of
antagonism make them.** A character is defined as much by what pushes back.
How values oppose each other, and how deep a reversal has to go before it
reaches the person, is in `values.md`.

## Cast

**The protagonist creates the rest of the cast.** Other characters exist
primarily in relation to the protagonist, each highlighting a dimension of the
protagonist's complexity.

- **Polarization, not redundancy.** Each role is designed by contrasting
  attitude; characters who repeat a dimension are wasted.
- A supporting character is not a smaller protagonist — they are a pressure
  the protagonist is measured against.

## Surface and depth

**Text is the sensory surface — what is shown or said. Subtext is the life
under it, the thoughts and feelings hidden by behavior.** Nothing is what it
seems; a character veils the truth with a living mask.

- Writing holds both layers at once: the sensory surface, and the inner world
  of desires, impulses and reactions behind it.
- The gap between the two is where meaning lives.

## Backstory

**Powerful revelations come from the backstory** — a significant past event
surfacing at a critical moment, and that moment becoming a turning point.

- Backstory is dramatized, not explained. It is a live scene, not a summary.
- Flashbacks enter only when the audience has a need and desire to know;
  bringing one before that desire exists is withholding, not mystery.
